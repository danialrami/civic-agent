# Why Past Solutions Haven't Worked

The existing ecosystem of voter information tools shares a common flaw: they all rely on a **pull-based model** where the voter must remember to check for information. This fundamental design choice, combined with other shortcomings, explains why local election participation remains low.

## The Pull vs. Push Problem

All existing solutions require the voter to take action:
1. Remember that an election is coming up
2. Navigate to a website
3. Enter their address
4. Find the relevant information
5. Research candidates manually

If a voter doesn't remember to check, they miss the election. There's no equivalent of a "calendar notification" that says "Early voting starts tomorrow for your primary runoff."

## No Personal Workflow Integration

Modern life runs on calendars:
- Work meetings → Google/Outlook calendar
- Doctor appointments → Calendar alerts
- Bill due dates → Banking app notifications
- **Elections → ???**

Existing tools don't integrate with daily workflows. A voter might see a Vote.org email and think "I'll check that later" and then forget. If that same election appeared as a calendar event with a reminder, it becomes part of their normal routine.

## Lack of Hyper-Local Nuance

National voting websites treat all voters the same. They don't account for:
- **Open primary strategies**: In Texas, voters can choose which party's primary to participate in. This is a tactical decision (e.g., "The Republican primary for AG is competitive, the Democratic one isn't—I'll vote Republican primary to influence that race").
- **Micro-district races**: A voter's specific address determines which school board district, city council district, and county commissioner precinct they vote in. Generic advice doesn't help.
- **Local propositions**: City-specific propositions (bond issues, charter amendments) only appear on certain ballots and are easy to overlook.

## Fragmented Information

To be fully informed, a voter currently must:
1. Check county website for election date and polling location
2. Visit Ballotpedia for candidate backgrounds
3. Google each candidate for recent news
4. Check candidate websites for platforms
5. Research propositions separately
6. Manually add dates to calendar

This fragmentation means most voters only do step 1 (if that), and skip the rest.

## The "Young Voter" Gap

Young voters are digital natives but have lower turnout. Why?
- They expect push notifications, not pull-based websites
- They move frequently, changing their voting jurisdiction
- They don't have habits built up around checking election sites
- They're less likely to receive mailers at their current address

## Trust and Accuracy Issues

- **Outdated information**: Candidate websites from previous elections still show up in search results
- **Partisan filtering**: Voter guides from parties omit relevant information
- **AI hallucinations**: As LLMs become more common for research, they sometimes fabricate candidate information or election dates

## Summary

Past solutions failed because they:
1. Require voters to remember to check (pull vs. push)
2. Don't integrate with daily workflows (no calendar sync)
3. Lack hyper-local personalization
4. Fragment information across multiple sources
5. Don't account for local voting nuances (open primaries, runoffs, micro-districts)
6. Fail to reach young voters where they are
